What Happens When People Keep Believing What Could Get Them Killed?

A Sociological Analysis of Christian Persecution in Northern Nigeria

The Question

On June 5, 2022 — Pentecost Sunday — gunmen stormed St. Francis Xavier Catholic Church in Owo, Nigeria, during Mass and opened fire, killing at least fifty people including children. Some attackers had disguised themselves as congregants to get inside. No ransom was demanded. No property was taken.

After the attack, something unexpected happened: the churches stayed open, the congregations kept gathering, and the faith kept spreading.

This pattern raises a fundamental question that sociology should be able to answer: Why do people continue to believe something that is literally getting them killed?

The Main Argument

Three major theories in sociology try to explain religion by saying it comes from something else — economic suffering (Marx), psychological need (Freud), or outdated thinking that should disappear as society modernizes (Secularization Theory).

The situation in Nigeria challenges all three theories. When we look at what's actually happening — churches growing even as Christians are being killed — these theories can't fully explain it. Religion isn't just a side effect of other problems. It has its own power that these theories miss.

Background: What's Happening in Nigeria

The Violence

Since 2009, the terrorist group Boko Haram has targeted Christians in northern Nigeria. In 2015, they pledged loyalty to ISIS. At the same time, conflicts between Muslim herders and Christian farmers have turned increasingly violent. Research shows that when herders and farmers share the same religion, conflicts are resolved peacefully. When they don't — when herders are Muslim and farmers are Christian — violence escalates.

The Response

Despite the danger, Christian communities continue to practice their faith openly:

  • A 20-year-old survivor named Andrew watched people he'd known for years — including children — shot in the aisles. His church did not close.
  • One congregation moved Sunday service to 7:00 AM for safety. Gunmen began their assault a few minutes after 7:00 AM. The congregation still meets.
  • Leah Sharibu, a schoolgirl kidnapped by Boko Haram in 2018, refused to convert to Islam while her Muslim classmates were released. She remained in captivity because of that refusal.

The Growth

Nigeria is projected to have the third largest Christian population in the world by 2050, with an estimated 211 million believers. This growth is happening in the same country, in the same era, in which more Christians are being killed for their faith than anywhere else on earth.

Three Theories, Three Different Explanations

Karl Marx

His Theory: Religion is both an expression of suffering and a protest against it — "the sigh of the oppressed creature."

What He Gets Right: Nigerian Christianity does express real suffering and functions as protest.

What He Misses: He can't explain why it keeps growing under persecution.

What Nigeria Shows Us

The persecution of Christians in northern Nigeria reveals something important: religion cannot be fully explained by reducing it to something else.

Marx sees it as a response to economic suffering. Freud sees it as psychological need. Secularization theorists see it as outdated thinking. All three assume religion is a side effect — that it exists because of other, more "real" forces.

But when people choose to keep believing something that could get them killed, when faith grows stronger under persecution rather than weaker, these explanations fall short. Religion has its own power that can't be explained away.

In the words of a second-century Christian named Tertullian, who watched Romans persecute his fellow believers: "The blood of Christians is seed." Two thousand years later, in northern Nigeria, that pattern holds.
