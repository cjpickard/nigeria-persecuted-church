Secularization Theory: Religion in Decline?

The theory that modernization makes religion disappear — and why Nigeria challenges it

What Secularization Theory Claims

"Once the world was filled with the sacred — in thought, practice, and institutional form. After the Reformation and the Renaissance, the forces of modernization swept across the globe and secularization, a corollary historical process, loosened the dominance of the sacred. In due course, the sacred shall disappear altogether except, possibly, in the private realm."

— C. Wright Mills, summarizing the secularization thesis (1959)

What This Means in Simple Terms

Secularization theory is one of the biggest ideas in sociology. For most of the 20th century, it was treated as obvious common sense. Here's the basic claim:

The Core Argument:

1. In the Past: The "Age of Faith"

Once upon a time (usually imagined as the Middle Ages in Europe), religion was everywhere. Everyone believed. The church controlled education, politics, social life, and culture. God was the explanation for everything from thunderstorms to diseases to why crops grew.

2. Then: Modernization Happened

Starting around the Renaissance and accelerating through the Enlightenment, several forces began to change society:

  • Science: Natural explanations replaced supernatural ones. We learned that thunder isn't God's anger; it's electricity in clouds.
  • Rationalization: People started thinking more logically, calculating costs and benefits, organizing society around efficiency rather than tradition.
  • Differentiation: Religion lost its monopoly. Churches were separated from the state. Education became secular. Medicine, law, and social welfare moved from church control to government or professional control.
  • Pluralism: People encountered other religions, which made it harder to believe that only one religion had the absolute truth.
3. The Result: Religion Declines

As societies modernize, they secularize. Religion becomes:

  • Less influential in public life
  • Less believable to educated people
  • More private and personal (if it survives at all)
  • Eventually, mostly irrelevant

According to this theory, religion is like belief in magic or monarchy — something humanity grows out of as it matures.

The Prediction:

In modern, rational, scientific, educated societies, religion will decline and eventually largely disappear.

This was considered so obvious that one sociologist (Anthony Wallace) predicted in 1966: "The evolutionary future of religion is extinction."

Secularization and Violence

There's an additional claim often attached to secularization theory: religion declines especially fast in societies experiencing violence and chaos.

The reasoning:

  • When violence and suffering increase, people pray for help
  • When help doesn't come (because God doesn't exist, according to secularization theorists), people lose faith
  • Prolonged violence and suffering therefore accelerate the decline of religion

By this logic, war, persecution, and systematic violence should speed up secularization.

Applying Secularization Theory to Nigeria

What the Theory Predicts for Nigeria

If secularization theory is correct, here's what should be happening in northern Nigeria:

  1. Religion should be declining overall as Nigeria modernizes (builds cities, develops technology, increases education)
  2. Religion should be declining especially fast among educated Nigerians who have been exposed to science and rational thinking
  3. The violence should accelerate the decline — as Christians pray for protection that doesn't come, they should lose faith
  4. Christianity should be collapsing in northern Nigeria specifically, where the persecution is most severe
  5. At minimum, Christianity should not be growing in areas where being Christian could get you killed

Bottom line: We should see Christianity in sharp decline, especially in the most dangerous areas.

What Actually Happens: Explosive Growth

The Data That Contradicts Secularization Theory:

211 million

Projected Christian population of Nigeria by 2050

This will make Nigeria the third largest Christian population in the world

#1

Nigeria ranks first in the world for Christian persecution

More Christians are being killed for their faith in Nigeria than anywhere else on earth

The Irony: The same country, in the same time period, is experiencing both the world's worst persecution of Christians AND massive growth in Christianity.

According to secularization theory, this should be impossible.

The Pattern Holds Historically:

This isn't a new phenomenon. In 197 AD, a Christian named Tertullian wrote to Roman authorities who were persecuting Christians:

"We multiply when you reap us. The blood of Christians is seed."

He was making an empirical observation: persecution was not stopping Christianity; it was spreading it. Watching people choose death over renunciation was producing converts, not deterring them.

Two thousand years later, in northern Nigeria, the same pattern holds.

The Research Evidence

Study: Asbury Theological Seminary

A doctoral study conducted at Asbury Theological Seminary, drawing on original research with Christian communities in northern Nigeria, found that:

Christians in the region overwhelmingly reported that pacifistic responses to persecution had a measurably positive impact on evangelism and discipleship, while retaliatory responses had a negative one.

In other words: The way persecuted Nigerian Christians have chosen to absorb violence without retaliating has been actively drawing people toward that faith rather than away from it.

This is the exact opposite of what secularization theory predicts. The theory says suffering should drive people away from religion. Instead, it's attracting them.

Why This Matters:

The new converts aren't ignorant of the cost. They're not being tricked. They know exactly what being Christian means in northern Nigeria — they've watched it happen to others. And they're choosing it anyway.

That suggests something secularization theory can't account for: people are choosing Christianity not despite the cost, but because they believe it's true.

What Went Wrong with Secularization Theory?

Problem 1: The "Age of Faith" Never Existed

Secularization theory depends on the idea that there was once an "Age of Faith" when everyone was deeply religious. But historians increasingly doubt this ever happened.

Dutch historian Peter Raedts argues that both the "Catholic Middle Ages" and the "Age of Reformation" are 19th-century myths. According to Raedts:

"A new era for Christianity in Europe began when after 1800 the churches gradually lost the support of the state and had to organize themselves. And it was not until then that the new mass media and the schooling of all the population made the christianization of everyone a reality."

In other words: The supposed "Age of Faith" may have actually been less religious than the 19th century. If there was never a golden age of universal belief, then there's no decline to explain.

The implications: Secularization theory is based on bad history. It compares modern religion not to the actual past, but to a romanticized, imaginary past that never existed.

Problem 2: The American Exception (That Wasn't an Exception)

For decades, secularization theorists explained away the United States by calling it "exceptional." Europe was secularizing as predicted, they said, but America was a weird outlier that would eventually catch up.

But the data from America was always a problem:

  • In every Gallup Poll since the 1940s, more than 90% of Americans say they believe in God
  • About 40% claim to attend religious services weekly (though this number is debated)
  • Religious membership in America was actually lower in the past than it is now:
    • 1776: Only 17% of Americans claimed religious membership
    • 1850: 34% claimed membership
    • Today: About 65% claim membership

The "Middletown Study" (1920s-1970s) tracked religious life in Muncie, Indiana over 50 years. Researchers expected to find evidence of secularization. Instead:

Of 15 measured indicators of religiosity, only 2 pointed downward (secularization). Three showed no trend. Ten (67%) showed an upward curve — greater religiosity in the 1970s than the 1920s.

America wasn't the exception. The theory was wrong.

Problem 3: Even Europe Isn't Secularizing the Way Predicted

While church attendance in Western Europe is indeed low, several complications challenge the secularization narrative:

  • State churches may have always had low participation. When church attendance is mandatory or taken for granted (because it's state-supported), people don't necessarily attend. When immigrants from Europe came to America where religion was voluntary, their participation often increased.
  • Alternative spiritualities are growing. People aren't necessarily becoming atheists; they're often shifting to different forms of religious or spiritual belief.
  • Immigration is re-sacralizing Europe. Muslim, Hindu, and Pentecostal Christian immigrants are bringing vibrant religious communities to supposedly secular Europe.

Peter Berger, one of the most famous proponents of secularization theory, renounced it in 1992:

"By the late 1970s it had been falsified with a vengeance. As it turned out, the theory never had much empirical substance to begin with."

Problem 4: The Theory Confused "Differentiation" with "Decline"

One thing that has happened in modern societies: functional differentiation. Religion is no longer in charge of education, healthcare, law, politics, and social welfare. Those functions have moved to specialized secular institutions.

Secularization theorists assumed: If religion loses these social functions, people will stop being religious.

But that doesn't follow. Religion might lose institutional power while religious belief remains strong or even grows. In fact, some scholars argue that when religion is freed from having to run schools and hospitals, it can focus on what it does best: providing meaning, community, and transcendent purpose.

As Rodney Stark points out: If differentiation really caused decline, we would expect to see less religion in modern societies than in medieval ones. But as the data shows, we often see more.

Problem 5: The Theory Treats Religion as Passive

Secularization theory sees religion as something that happens to people based on external social forces:

  • If society is traditional and pre-modern → people are religious
  • If society modernizes → people stop being religious

But this makes religion entirely passive and reactive. It can't explain:

  • Why people choose religion when it's costly
  • Why religion sometimes creates social change rather than just responding to it
  • Why religious revivals happen
  • Why new religious movements emerge

Nigeria demonstrates religion's active power: Christianity isn't just surviving in spite of persecution; it's using persecution (through the pacifistic response) as a form of evangelism. The religion is actively shaping events, not passively responding to them.

An Alternative: Secularization as a Self-Limiting Process

Sociologists Rodney Stark and William Sims Bainbridge proposed a different model: secularization as a self-limiting process.

The Core Idea:

Yes, some religions do decline — particularly when they become too comfortable, too compromised with secular power, or too watered-down in their teachings. But when old religions decline, new ones emerge.

Why Religion Keeps Coming Back:

  1. Existential questions don't go away: Death, suffering, meaning, purpose — these questions remain even in modern societies. Science can tell us how things work, but it can't tell us why we're here or what we should do with our lives.
  2. Two responses to secularization:
    • Revival movements within existing traditions try to recover the original, more demanding faith (think: Pentecostalism in Christianity, or fundamentalism in various religions)
    • New religious movements emerge from outside the existing traditions, offering fresh answers to eternal questions
  3. The cycle continues: Some of these new movements will themselves eventually secularize and decline, leading to new revivals and new movements.

Result: Religion doesn't disappear. It changes form. Some traditions decline while others grow. But the total amount of religion remains relatively constant or even increases.

How This Applies to Nigeria:

Nigerian Christianity isn't just surviving; it's thriving precisely because it's high-demand religion. It asks everything of its followers — potentially including their lives. And paradoxically, that's part of its appeal.

The pacifistic response to persecution isn't weakness; it's a powerful statement: We believe this is true enough to die for, and we believe it's true enough that we won't betray it even to save ourselves.

That kind of costly, committed faith attracts people in a way that comfortable, culturally-established religion never could.

What Secularization Theory Gets Right, What It Gets Wrong

✓ What Secularization Theory Explains Well

  • Why some traditional religious institutions have lost social power
  • Why religion is no longer in charge of education, medicine, law, etc.
  • Why some people (especially in Europe) have left organized religion
  • Why pluralism makes absolute religious claims harder to maintain

✗ What Secularization Theory Cannot Explain

  • Why religious belief remains high in modern societies like the U.S.
  • Why religion grows under persecution instead of declining
  • Why new religious movements keep emerging
  • Why people choose costly, demanding religions
  • Why the "Age of Faith" may have never existed
  • Why religion sometimes increases as societies modernize

The Verdict: Secularization theory was built on bad history (the myth of the Age of Faith), bad predictions (religion should disappear), and bad assumptions (religion is passive, not active).

Northern Nigeria is the perfect test case because it combines multiple factors that should, according to the theory, cause religion to collapse:

  • Modernization (cities, technology, education)
  • Pluralism (multiple religions in conflict)
  • Violence and suffering (prayers going unanswered)
  • Rational cost-benefit analysis (being Christian could get you killed)

Theory predicts: Religion should be in sharp decline, especially in persecution zones.

Reality shows: Religion is experiencing explosive growth, especially in persecution zones.

This doesn't just challenge secularization theory. It demolishes it. And it suggests that religion has a power and persistence that purely sociological theories — theories that treat religion as a byproduct of other forces — simply cannot capture.
