Works Cited

Academic sources, news reports, and theoretical readings

Primary Analytical Paper

Pickard, Charles. "The Witness of Nigeria's Persecuted Church." Analytical Paper, Sociology of Religion, Dr. Rebecca Bartel, April 12, 2026.

News & Current Events

Associated Press. "Gunmen Kill Dozens at Catholic Church in Nigeria During Pentecost Sunday Mass." AP News, June 5, 2022. Link

Primary news source documenting the Owo church massacre on Pentecost Sunday 2022, including survivor testimony and casualty counts.

Academic Sources: Nigeria Context

Heft, Annelore, Emily Hana Mayweg-Paus, and Katharina Kleinen-von Königslöw. "Understanding News Coverage of Religious-based Violence: Empirical and Theoretical Insights from Media Representations of Boko Haram in Nigeria." Journal of Communication, vol. 70, no. 4, Aug. 2020, pp. 548–572.

Analyzes how Nigerian media across religious divides struggles to frame Boko Haram conflict in purely material terms because theological dimensions keep reasserting themselves in reporting.

Je'Adayibe, Gwamna Dogara. "Early Christian Responses to Persecution and Their Implications to Contemporary Churches in Nigeria." Doctoral dissertation, Asbury Theological Seminary, 2019.

Key empirical research finding that pacifistic responses to persecution in northern Nigeria had measurably positive impact on evangelism, while retaliatory responses had negative impact.

Tuki, Daniel. "Is There a Religious Dimension to Concern about Farmer-Herder Conflicts in Nigeria?" HiCN Working Paper 391, Households in Conflict Network, Apr. 2023.

Instrumental variable regression analysis showing that shared Islam between herders and farmers significantly reduces likelihood of violent conflict; when religious identity differs (Muslim herders, Christian farmers), resource competition hardens into religious conflict.

Theoretical Sources: Karl Marx

Marx, Karl. "Towards a Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right: Introduction" (1843). In Marx on Religion, edited by John Raines. Philadelphia: Temple University Press, 2002.

Contains the famous "opium of the people" passage. Marx argues religious suffering is simultaneously expression of real suffering and protest against it — religion as "sigh of the oppressed creature."

McKinnon, Andrew M. "Reading 'Opium of the People': Expression, Protest and the Dialectics of Religion." Critical Sociology, vol. 31, issues 1-2, 2005.

Argues that Marx's "opium" metaphor in mid-19th century had multiple meanings (medicine, source of profit, cause of conflict, source of utopian visions) that complicate simple interpretations of religion as purely passive or conservative force.

Theoretical Sources: Sigmund Freud

Freud, Sigmund. The Future of an Illusion (1927). In The Standard Edition of the Complete Psychological Works of Sigmund Freud, vol. 21, edited by James Strachey. London: Hogarth Press, 1961.

Freud's main work on religion, arguing that religious beliefs are illusions based on wish-fulfillment — projections of the protective father figure onto the universe to cope with human helplessness against nature.

Pals, Daniel L. "Religion and Personality: Sigmund Freud." In Eight Theories of Religion, 2nd ed. New York: Oxford University Press, 2006, pp. 53-83.

Clear summary of Freud's psychoanalytic approach to religion, including the Oedipus complex, religion as neurosis, and the concept of projection. Includes critique of Freud's reductionism and circular reasoning.

Theoretical Sources: Secularization Theory

Swatos, William H., Jr., and Kevin J. Christiano. "Secularization Theory: The Course of a Concept." Sociology of Religion, vol. 60, no. 3, Autumn 1999, pp. 209-228.

Comprehensive overview of secularization theory from 1960s to 1990s, documenting its rise as sociological dogma and subsequent empirical challenges. Discusses myth of "Age of Faith" and problems with evolutionary assumptions.

Hadden, Jeffrey K. "Toward Desacralizing Secularization Theory." Social Forces, vol. 65, 1987, pp. 587-611.

Landmark critique arguing secularization constitutes doctrine more than theory, based on presuppositions representing taken-for-granted ideology rather than systematic propositions. Shows theory lacks empirical support after 20+ years of research.

Berger, Peter L. "Secularism in Retreat." National Interest, vol. 46, Winter 1996-1997, pp. 3-12.

Influential recantation by former secularization proponent: "By the late 1970s it had been falsified with a vengeance. As it turned out, the theory never had much empirical substance to begin with."

Stark, Rodney, and William Sims Bainbridge. The Future of Religion: Secularization, Revival, and Cult Formation. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1985.

Proposes alternative to linear secularization: religion as self-limiting process. When old religions decline, new movements emerge because existential questions persist. Shows that lower traditional religious practice correlates with higher new religious movement activity.

Finke, Roger, and Rodney Stark. The Churching of America, 1776-2005. New Brunswick, NJ: Rutgers University Press, 1992.

Historical data showing U.S. religious membership was only 17% in 1776, 34% in 1850, and over 60% today — opposite of secularization predictions. Argues religious competition increases participation rather than decreasing it.

Historical Context

Raedts, Peter. Quoted in W. de Ruyter, "Dark, backward, and barbarous." Leiden Institute for the Study of Religions Newsletter, vol. 1, 1996, pp. 3-8.

Argues the "Catholic Middle Ages" and "Age of Reformation" are 19th-century creations; serious Christianization of entire European population didn't begin until after 1800 with mass media and universal schooling.

Tertullian. Apologeticus (197 AD). Quoted in various sources.

Early Christian apologist who observed that Roman persecution was failing to stop Christianity: "We multiply when you reap us. The blood of Christians is seed" — making empirical observation that martyrdom produced converts rather than deterring them.

Supporting Studies

Caplow, Theodore, Howard M. Bahr, and Bruce A. Chadwick. All Faithful People: Change and Continuity in Middletown's Religion. Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 1983.

Follow-up to 1920s-1930s Middletown studies. Of 15 religiosity indicators tracked 1924-1978, only 2 showed decline; 10 (67%) showed increased religiosity. Contradicted secularization predictions.

Greeley, Andrew M. Religious Change in America. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1989.

Survey data showing consistent high levels of American religious belief and practice from 1940s to 1980s: 90%+ believe in God, 70%+ pray regularly, 40% attend weekly services. Little change over 50 years despite modernization.
