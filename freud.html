Sigmund Freud: Religion as Wish-Fulfillment

Understanding religion as a response to human helplessness

What Freud Said About Religion

"Religion would thus be the universal obsessional neurosis of humanity; like the obsessional neurosis of children, it arose out of the Oedipus Complex, out of the relation to the father."

— Sigmund Freud, "The Future of an Illusion" (1927)

What This Means in Simple Terms

Freud had a much harsher view of religion than Marx. Where Marx saw religion as both a symptom and a protest, Freud saw it as essentially a mental illness — specifically, a neurosis.

His Main Arguments:

1. Humans Are Helpless Against Nature

Nature constantly threatens to destroy us — through storms, disease, predators, accidents, aging, and death. We join together in societies (civilizations) for protection, but even civilization can't fully protect us. Eventually, disease and death always win.

2. We Want What We Had as Children

As children, we had a father who made us feel safe. When we were scared of the storm or the dark, our father reassured us that everything would be okay. As adults, we still crave that feeling of safety, but we can't have it anymore.

3. Religion Gives Us an Imaginary Father

Religion projects onto the universe a God who acts like that protective father. This God:

  • Has power over nature
  • Watches over each person individually
  • Promises that death isn't really the end
  • Rewards us for being good

According to Freud, this is wish-fulfillment — we believe it because we desperately want it to be true, not because we have good evidence for it.

4. Religion Is "Illusion" (Maybe Even "Delusion")

Freud makes a distinction:

  • Illusion: A belief we hold mainly because we want it to be true (it might happen to be true, but that's not why we believe it)
  • Delusion: A belief we hold that we know is false (and probably can never be true)

Freud officially calls religion an "illusion," but he strongly hints it's actually a delusion. He just can't definitively prove God doesn't exist, so he sticks with the more cautious term.

5. Religion Is Childhood for Humanity

Just as individual people go through childhood before becoming adults, humanity as a whole goes through stages. Religion belongs to the childhood of the human race. As we mature, we should — and eventually will — leave it behind, just as we leave behind childhood fears of the dark.

Freud writes: "Religion would thus be the universal obsessional neurosis of humanity... If this view is right, it is to be supposed that a turning-away from religion is bound to occur with the fatal inevitability of a process of growth."

The Core Claim:

People create God because they're scared and helpless, not because God is real. Religion is a comforting story we tell ourselves to feel safer in a dangerous world.

Applying Freud to Nigeria

What Freud Would Predict

If Freud's theory is correct, here's what we should see in northern Nigeria:

  1. Initial turn to religion: When persecution begins, people feel helpless and turn to God for comfort and protection (wish-fulfillment kicks in)
  2. Growing disillusionment: As the attacks continue and God doesn't intervene to stop them, people start to lose faith
  3. Eventual abandonment: When it becomes clear that being Christian actually makes you less safe, not more, people abandon the religion for something that offers real protection (or at least doesn't make them a target)

In short: Christian faith in northern Nigeria should be declining, probably rapidly.

What Actually Happens: The Opposite

The Evidence That Contradicts Freud:

1. Faith Persists Despite Providing No Physical Safety

Andrew crouched under a seat with his mother and watched people he'd known for years — including children running toward their parents — fall in the aisles in their Sunday clothes. His church did not close.

If religion exists to make people feel safe, why would Andrew keep attending a church where he watched fifty people get murdered? The "protective father" God clearly did not protect them. And yet the faith continues.

2. People Choose Faith Even When It Increases Danger

Consider Leah Sharibu, the schoolgirl kidnapped by Boko Haram. Her Muslim classmates were released. She was told she would be released too — if she converted to Islam. She refused and remained in captivity.

Freud's theory says religion is about wish-fulfillment, about wanting safety and comfort. Leah's wish was clearly to go home and be safe. Her religion prevented that. Yet she chose her religion over her safety.

This is the exact opposite of what Freud predicts.

3. New People Join the Dangerous Religion

The most devastating evidence against Freud: the religion is growing.

Nigeria is projected to have the third largest Christian population in the world by 2050, with an estimated 211 million believers. This growth is occurring in the same country, in the same era, in which more Christians are being killed for their faith than anywhere else on earth.

People are converting to Christianity while watching Christians get killed for being Christian.

If Freud is right — if religion exists because it makes people feel safe — this makes no sense whatsoever.

4. The "Wish" Is Not for Safety

Research from Asbury Theological Seminary found that Christians in northern Nigeria overwhelmingly reported that pacifistic responses to persecution had a measurably positive impact on evangelism and discipleship. In other words, the way persecuted Nigerian Christians have chosen to absorb violence — without renouncing their faith and largely without retaliating — has been actively drawing people toward that faith.

What are these new converts "wishing" for? Not safety. Not comfort. Not a father-figure who will protect them from harm.

They're wishing for something Freud's theory doesn't account for: the belief that their faith is true and worth dying for.

The Fatal Flaw in Freud's Theory

Freud Assumes Religion Is About Feeling Safe

The entire structure of Freud's argument rests on this premise: people believe in God because it makes them feel protected in a dangerous world.

But northern Nigeria proves this premise is false.

In most social phenomena, when the cost of participation rises high enough, participation declines. That's basic rational behavior. If going to church could get you killed, most people would stop going to church — at least, if their only reason for going was to feel safe and comforted.

"Most social phenomena, when the cost of participation rises high enough, see participation decline. That is the rational calculus. What the witness of the Nigerian church demonstrates is that a significant number of people — a growing number — are operating out of a framework in which what they believe is more real and more important than their physical survival."

Two Possibilities:

  1. Freud is wrong: Religion is not primarily about wish-fulfillment and feeling safe. It's about something else — perhaps a genuine belief that what they're worshiping is real and true.
  2. Nigerian Christians are all deeply irrational: They're clinging to a neurotic illusion even when it's obviously not providing what it's supposed to provide (safety and comfort).

Freud would have to take option 2. But option 2 requires us to believe that hundreds of thousands of people — including children like Leah Sharibu making clear-headed decisions about what matters most — are all suffering from the same delusion.

Option 1 is more plausible: Freud misunderstood what religion is and why people believe.

Freud Can't Distinguish Between Neurosis and Conviction

Freud argues that religious behavior looks like obsessional neurosis: both involve rituals, guilt, and irrational beliefs. A neurotic person washes their hands 50 times a day; a religious person prays 5 times a day. Both are engaged in compulsive behavior that doesn't make rational sense.

The problem: Context matters.

A nun who spends hours at prayer moving her prayer beads and a neurotic who spends hours counting the buttons on his shirt are both engaged in the same form of behavior, but only one is mentally ill. For nuns, prayer is normal, not neurotic.

Similarly, a Christian in northern Nigeria who continues attending church despite the danger is not necessarily neurotic. They may simply believe — deeply and rationally — that their faith is true and that truth is worth the cost.

Freud's framework cannot tell the difference between:

  • Someone clinging to an illusion because they're psychologically weak
  • Someone holding to a conviction because they believe it's true

He assumes all religious belief is the first. Nigeria suggests it might be the second.

What Freud Gets Right, What He Misses

✓ What Freud Explains Well

  • Why people turn to religion when feeling vulnerable
  • Why the idea of a protective God appeals to humans
  • Why religion often provides comfort in the face of death
  • Why some religious beliefs might be wishful thinking

✗ What Freud Cannot Explain

  • Why people keep believing when religion makes them less safe
  • Why people join a religion that increases their danger
  • Why faith grows under persecution instead of declining
  • Why people choose death over renouncing their beliefs
  • The difference between neurotic illusion and rational conviction

The Bottom Line: Freud sees religion as a crutch for weak, scared people who can't face reality. His theory predicts that when the crutch fails to provide actual support — when God doesn't protect you from gunmen — people will throw it away.

Northern Nigeria shows the opposite. When the cost of faith becomes life itself, many people don't abandon their beliefs. Instead, they hold them even more firmly. And others, watching this, are drawn to join them.

This suggests that whatever religion is, it's not primarily about psychological comfort or wish-fulfillment. It's about something deeper — something Freud's reductionist psychology misses entirely.
